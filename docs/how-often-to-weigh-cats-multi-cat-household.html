How Often Should You Weigh Each Cat in a Multi-Cat Household?

← Back to the Complete Multi-Cat Household Guide

October 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Healthy adults: weigh each cat once a month.
  • Kittens, seniors and cats with a health concern: weigh every one to two weeks.
  • Watch the percentage: a 5% loss over a month or two is worth mentioning to your vet; 10% or more deserves a conversation sooner.
  • Be consistent: same method, same time of day, and write it down by cat every time.

The Short Answer: Once a Month, Per Cat

For most healthy adult cats in a multi-cat household, weighing each cat once a month is enough to catch meaningful changes without turning weighing into a chore.

CatHow often to weigh
Healthy adultOnce a month
KittenEvery one to two weeks
SeniorEvery one to two weeks
Any cat with an ongoing health concernEvery one to two weeks

Kittens, seniors and cats with ongoing concerns benefit from more frequent weighing because their weight can shift faster, and those shifts matter more. The exact interval matters less than doing it consistently and recording the number by cat, every time. That way a slow drift over months doesn't disappear into "I think she looks about the same."

Weight is one of the few observations that doesn't depend on which cat you happened to notice at the food bowl that day. A scale gives you a number, and a number is much harder to talk yourself out of than an impression. That's why it's worth building into your tracking routine rather than only thinking about it when a cat already looks different.

Why Multi-Cat Households Lose Track of Weight

Weight loss or gain in a single cat is often the slowest, quietest change there is. A cat can lose ten percent of its body weight over two or three months without anyone noticing.

  • It happens gradually. The change comes a little at a time.
  • Fur hides it. Fur conceals a surprising amount of frame change.
  • Handling gets split. In a one-cat home, owners sometimes catch changes through daily handling. In a multi-cat home, that handling is split three or four ways.
  • Shared bowls mislead. A cat that's quietly losing weight might still be at the bowl, nudging in and appearing to eat, while a more dominant cat eats most of what's there.

Watching bowls tells you food is disappearing. Weighing cats individually tells you whether that food is actually reaching the cat who needs it.

What Counts as a Meaningful Change

Small week-to-week fluctuations of a few ounces are normal and not worth worrying about on their own. Hydration, a recent meal and even which way the cat is facing on the scale can shift a reading slightly. What matters is a consistent direction over time.

ChangeWhat it means
A few ounces, week to weekNormal fluctuation
5% of body weight lost over a month or twoGenerally considered worth mentioning to your vet
10% or more lostA stronger signal that something has changed; talk to your vet sooner rather than later

The percentage matters more than the raw number, because it scales with the cat. A half-pound loss means something very different for a six-pound cat than for a sixteen-pound cat.

Data, not impressions

"The cats seem a little thinner" isn't a data point. "Mocha went from 9.8 to 9.1 pounds over six weeks" is something you and your vet can actually act on. This is why per-cat logging matters so much in a multi-cat household.

How to Weigh Multiple Cats Without a Fight

You don't need a veterinary scale. Choose the method that suits each cat:

  • Bathroom scale, held. Weigh yourself holding the cat, then weigh yourself alone, and subtract. Works for most cats.
  • Bathroom scale, in a carrier. For cats who won't be held still, weigh the carrier with the cat inside, then the empty carrier, and subtract.
  • Baby or kitchen scale. For small or very cooperative cats, a baby scale or kitchen scale with a towel gives a more precise reading.

Keep the method the same

Pick a consistent time of day and a consistent method for each cat. Weighing after a meal versus before one, or in a carrier versus held, can shift the number by a few ounces, and you don't want to mistake that for real change. Consistency within each cat's own measurements matters more than perfect precision.

Building Weigh-Ins Into Your Routine

The easiest way to keep up with monthly weighing is to anchor it to something that already happens regularly:

  • The first weekend of the month
  • The day you refill a certain food bag
  • A recurring calendar reminder

Weigh each cat on the same day if you can, so you're comparing a full household snapshot rather than piecing together numbers from different weeks.

If a cat is already showing other signs worth watching, such as reduced appetite, more hiding or changes in litter box habits, move that cat to every-two-week weighing temporarily, even if the rest of the household stays monthly. You don't need everyone on the same frequency. The schedule should follow what each cat actually needs.

Why Writing It Down Changes Everything

A number you remember from "a few months ago" is not a number you can trust. Memory rounds, memory forgets the exact month, and memory is especially unreliable when you're comparing several cats at once.

  1. Write down the date, the cat and the weight. Every time. "Has she lost weight?" becomes a question you can answer in seconds.
  2. Log it alongside other observations. Appetite, litter box activity and behavior notes give each number context.
  3. Look at the context when a trend appears. Did appetite drop around the same time? Did activity change? Is it one cat or the whole household?

That context is what turns a single weigh-in into a pattern your veterinarian can actually use.
