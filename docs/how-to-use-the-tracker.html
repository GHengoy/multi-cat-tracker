Walking Through Each Column

Date: The calendar date of your observation. For example, "August 21, 2026" or however you prefer to format dates. This is the timestamp that organizes everything else. If you make multiple observations in a day, you might create multiple rows for the same date with different times or times in the "Notes" column.

Notes: A brief label for context. Examples: "Morning check," "After breakfast," "Midday," "Evening review." This helps you and your veterinarian understand what time of day this observation was made and gives context for the entries. It's especially useful when reviewing with your vet, as it clarifies whether an observation is a snapshot or represents a full day of activity.

Cat Name: The name of the specific cat you're logging this observation for. This is the column that makes multi-cat tracking actually work—it isolates each cat's patterns individually. Write the cat's name (e.g., "Whiskers," "Shadow," "Luna") consistently throughout the tracker. This consistency makes it easy to search and filter your entries by individual cat later.

Litter Box Visits: How many times you observed this specific cat using the litter box today (or during the time period noted). Just put a number: "4," "2," "6." If you observe the same cat using the box multiple times, add them up. If you didn't see the cat use the box, you can put "0 observed" or leave it blank. This column alone often catches the first signs that something has shifted in a cat's routine.

Stool Quality: A simple description of what you observe in the litter box for this cat. Examples: "normal," "soft," "hard," "clumped," "loose." Don't interpret—just describe what you see. This is one of the most useful columns for noticing changes, and having it written down makes discussion with your veterinarian concrete and specific. Your vet can now ask, "For how many days was it like that?" and you have your tracker to refer to.

Appetite Level: What you observe about how this cat is eating. Write: "normal" (ate regular portions), "reduced" (ate less than usual), "enthusiastic" (seemed very eager), or "refused" (didn't eat offered food). Again, you're describing what you see, not interpreting. This simple descriptor builds a picture of each cat's eating patterns over time.

Water Intake (ml): An approximate amount of water this cat drank if you can estimate it. You don't need to be exact—this column is for tracking patterns, not laboratory precision. You might measure by refilling a bowl and noting how much is gone, or simply estimate: "about 100ml," "normal amount," "high," "low." If you can't measure precisely, just describe what you observed compared to normal. The point is to track whether one cat is drinking more or less than usual over a week or two.

Medication Given: If this cat received any medication, supplement, probiotic, special food, or other item you gave them, note it here. Examples: "Thyroid pill," "Probiotic," "Special diet meal," "None," "Pain relief supplement." This column is crucial if you're managing cats on medications or dietary adjustments, because it creates a clear record of what was given and when. You can later match these entries against observations in other columns to see if there's a relationship.

Activity Level: What you observe about this cat's movement and energy. Examples: "active" (playing, running, jumping), "normal" (typical movement for this cat), "lethargic" (moving less, resting more), "restless," "playful." Describe what you actually see. Some cats are naturally less active than others, so "normal" is what normal looks like for that specific cat, not compared to other cats or to some standard.

Behavior Notes: Any other observations about this cat's behavior or routine. Examples: "grooming normally," "hiding more than usual," "meowing frequently," "spending more time in sunny window," "sleeping in favorite spot," "seeking human attention," "avoiding the other cats." This column captures things that don't fit into the structured columns but are still worth noting. Write what you observe without interpretation. Your vet will interpret it.

Food Amount (grams): The approximate amount of food (in grams) you offered this cat at meals. If you feed measured portions, just write the amount: "80g," "120g." If you feed free-choice or by eye, write a description like "normal portion" or "half usual amount." This helps track feeding consistency and can be valuable information when discussing dietary changes with your veterinarian.

Real-World Example

Here's what a day might look like in your tracker. Let's say you have two cats—Whiskers and Shadow:

Row 1: Date: August 21, Notes: Morning, Cat: Whiskers, Litter: 2, Stool: Normal, Appetite: Normal, Water: ~80ml, Medication: None, Activity: Active, Behavior: Playing with toy, Food: 100g

Row 2: Date: August 21, Notes: Morning, Cat: Shadow, Litter: 1, Stool: Normal, Appetite: Normal, Water: ~60ml, Medication: None, Activity: Normal, Behavior: Resting by window, Food: 100g

Row 3: Date: August 21, Notes: Evening, Cat: Whiskers, Litter: 2, Stool: Normal, Appetite: Normal, Water: ~40ml, Medication: None, Activity: Normal, Behavior: Grooming, Food: 100g

Row 4: Date: August 21, Notes: Evening, Cat: Shadow, Litter: 1, Stool: Normal, Appetite: Reduced, Water: Not observed, Medication: None, Activity: Lethargic, Behavior: Hiding in bedroom more, Food: 50g

One evening entry shows Shadow with reduced appetite, less activity, and spending more time hiding. On its own, this might be nothing—cats have off days. But if you continue logging and see this pattern continue into the next day or two, you suddenly have data worth bringing to your veterinarian.

Why This Format Works

The tracker is designed so that each row represents one cat at one point in time. This makes it easy to pull together a week of observations for one cat and see if patterns emerge. It also makes it simple to share relevant sections with your veterinarian. You can print out just the rows for one cat, or just one week, to discuss specific concerns.
