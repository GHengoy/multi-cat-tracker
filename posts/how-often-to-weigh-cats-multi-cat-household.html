The Short Answer: Once a Month, Per Cat

For most healthy adult cats living in a multi-cat household, weighing each cat once a month is enough to catch meaningful changes without turning weighing into a chore. Kittens, seniors, and any cat with an ongoing health concern benefit from more frequent weighing—every one to two weeks—because their weight can shift faster and those shifts matter more. The exact interval matters less than doing it consistently and recording the number by cat, every time, so a slow drift over months doesn't disappear into "I think she looks about the same."

Weight is one of the few observations that doesn't depend on which cat you happened to notice at the food bowl that day. A scale gives you a number, and a number is much harder to talk yourself out of than an impression. That's exactly why it's worth building into your household's tracking routine rather than treating it as something you only think about when a cat already looks different.

Why Multi-Cat Households Lose Track of Weight Specifically

Weight loss or gain in a single cat is often the slowest, quietest change there is. A cat can lose ten percent of its body weight over two or three months without anyone noticing, because the change happens a little at a time and because fur hides a surprising amount of frame change. In a one-cat home, owners sometimes catch this through daily handling. In a multi-cat home, that daily handling gets split three or four ways, and subtle changes in any one cat are easy to miss entirely.

There's also a specific multi-cat problem: shared food bowls make it hard to know who's actually eating enough. A cat that's quietly losing weight might still be present at the bowl, nudging in, appearing to eat—while a more dominant cat is eating most of what's there. Watching bowls tells you food is disappearing. Weighing cats individually tells you whether that food is actually reaching the cat who needs it.

What Counts as a Meaningful Change

Small week-to-week fluctuations of a few ounces are normal and not worth worrying about on their own—hydration, a recent meal, and even which way the cat is facing on the scale can shift a reading slightly. What's worth paying attention to is a consistent direction over time. A loss of five percent of body weight over a month or two is generally considered worth mentioning to your vet. A loss of ten percent or more is a stronger signal that something has changed and deserves a conversation sooner rather than later.

The percentage matters more than the raw number, because it scales with the cat. A half-pound loss means something very different for a six-pound cat than it does for a sixteen-pound cat. This is one of the reasons per-cat logging matters so much in a multi-cat household—"the cats seem a little thinner" isn't a data point, but "Mocha went from 9.8 to 9.1 pounds over six weeks" is something you and your vet can actually act on.

How to Actually Weigh Multiple Cats Without a Fight

You don't need a veterinary scale. A standard bathroom scale works for most cats: weigh yourself holding the cat, then weigh yourself alone, and subtract. For cats who won't tolerate being held still, a cat carrier works the same way—weigh the carrier with the cat inside, then weigh the empty carrier afterward, and subtract. For small or very cooperative cats, a baby scale or kitchen scale with a towel gives a more precise reading.

Pick a consistent time of day and a consistent method for each cat, since the method itself can introduce small variations you don't want to mistake for real change. Weighing after a meal versus before one, or in a carrier versus held, can shift the number by a few ounces. Consistency within each cat's own measurements matters more than perfect precision.

Building Weigh-Ins Into Your Existing Routine

The easiest way to keep up with monthly weighing is to anchor it to something that already happens regularly—the first weekend of the month, the day you refill a certain food bag, or a recurring calendar reminder. Weigh each cat on the same day if you can, so you're comparing a full household snapshot rather than piecing together numbers from different weeks.

If a cat is already showing other signs worth watching—reduced appetite, more hiding, changes in litter box habits—shift that cat to every-two-week weighing temporarily, even if the rest of the household stays on a monthly schedule. You don't need to weigh everyone on the same frequency. The schedule should follow what each cat actually needs, not a single rule applied uniformly.

Why Writing It Down Changes Everything

A number you remember from "a few months ago" is not a number you can trust. Memory rounds, memory forgets the exact month, and memory is especially unreliable when you're comparing across several cats at once. Writing down the date, the cat, and the weight every time turns a vague sense of "has she lost weight?" into an actual answer you can check in seconds.

This is precisely the kind of observation a simple tracker is built for. Logging weight alongside appetite, litter box activity, and behavior notes means that when a number does start trending in one direction, you already have the surrounding context—whether appetite dropped around the same time, whether activity level changed, whether it's one cat or the whole household. That context is what turns a single weigh-in into a pattern your veterinarian can actually use.
