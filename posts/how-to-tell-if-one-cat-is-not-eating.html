Key takeaways

  • Shared bowls hide the answer: an empty bowl only tells you the group ate, not that every cat did.
  • Watch one meal closely: scheduled, supervised meals show you who eats, how much and who gets pushed away.
  • Look for indirect signs: a cat who hangs back, skips treats or changes litter box output is worth a closer look.
  • Act on timing: a cat who has eaten little or nothing for about a day deserves a call to your vet.
  • Write it down per cat: a simple appetite note at each meal turns a vague worry into a clear pattern.

Why an Empty Bowl Doesn't Mean Everyone Ate

In a one-cat home, food left in the bowl is an instant signal. In a multi-cat home, a bigger appetite can quietly cover for a smaller one. If one cat stops eating, another cat may happily finish their share, and the bowl still looks empty by evening.

This is why appetite changes are among the easiest things to miss when you live with several cats. Cats also tend to hide when they feel unwell, so the cat eating less is often the one you notice least. The goal is to stop judging the group and start checking each cat.

Switch to Scheduled, Supervised Meals

The most reliable way to know who is eating is to watch them eat. Free-feeding from a shared bowl makes that nearly impossible, so moving to set mealtimes, even for a week or two, gives you a clear answer.

  1. Serve two or three set meals a day. Put food down at the same times and pick it up after 20 to 30 minutes.
  2. Give every cat their own bowl. Space the bowls apart, or feed in separate rooms, so no cat can crowd another out.
  3. Measure what goes down. Use the same scoop or a kitchen scale so "ate half" means the same thing every day.
  4. Check each bowl when you pick it up. Note roughly how much each cat left: all, most, half, a little or none.

If you can't supervise every meal

Watch the first meal of the day closely and let the rest run as usual. One supervised meal daily still tells you far more than an empty shared bowl at night.

Signs One Cat Is Eating Less

Even without watching every meal, a few behaviors tend to point to the cat who is not eating as much as usual.

  • Hanging back at mealtime: a cat who used to rush the kitchen now waits, wanders off or shows up after the others have finished.
  • Skipping treats: turning down a favorite treat is one of the clearest everyday appetite signals.
  • Sniffing and walking away: approaching food but leaving without eating is different from simply not being hungry yet.
  • Less in the litter box: smaller or fewer stools from one cat often follow reduced eating within a day or two.
  • Weight or body changes: a slightly bonier back or hips, or a drop on the scale, can confirm a slow decline.
  • Hiding or sleeping more: a quieter, more withdrawn cat is worth checking on at the next meal.

Rule Out Food Competition

Sometimes a cat isn't losing appetite at all; they're being pushed away from the food. A confident cat may guard bowls, eat fast and move on to others' portions, or simply sit near the feeding area so a shyer cat won't approach.

The quickest test is simple: feed the cat you're worried about alone in a quiet room for a meal or two. If they eat normally there, the fix is likely the feeding setup, such as more spaced-out stations or separate rooms, rather than the cat.

When a Change in Appetite Needs Your Vet

Cats shouldn't go long without eating. A cat who has eaten little or nothing for about 24 hours deserves a call to your vet, and sooner for kittens, seniors or cats with an existing health condition. Don't wait to see if it passes over several days.

Call sooner if you also notice

Vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, hiding, drooling, straining in the litter box or a noticeable drop in weight alongside eating less. Your vet can decide what needs checking; your job is to notice and report clearly.

When you call, specifics help: which cat, roughly how much they've eaten at each meal, when it started and anything else that changed. That is far more useful than "one of them seems off."

Log Appetite Per Cat, Every Meal

Appetite notes take seconds, but they only work if they're tied to a specific cat. A quick rating at each meal builds a baseline, so you'll notice when one cat drifts from their normal instead of relying on a vague impression.

  • Use a simple scale: all, most, half, a little or none is enough detail.
  • Note the context: new food, a visitor, a moved bowl or a stressful day.
  • Review weekly: look for a cat whose ratings slide over several days, not just one off meal.
  • Pair it with weight and litter notes: appetite, weight and litter box output together tell a much clearer story.
